# Three-Phase Blueprint for Increasing Vertical Jump and Dunking

> **Source:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JVQW9H7gc0o
> **Date:** 2026-05-30

## Core Principles

- Genetics influences jumping ability, but improvement is always within your control regardless of starting point
- The body adapts only as much as it needs to survive its environment — variety in training is required to prevent plateaus
- Getting brutally strong is non-negotiable; being "too strong" while jumping regularly is essentially impossible
- Failure only occurs if you quit — PRs can come years after apparent plateaus
- Shorter athletes must be proportionally stronger than taller athletes to achieve similar vertical heights

---

## Key Frameworks & Progressions

### Three-Phase Vertical Jump Development

**Phase 1 — Build the Engine (Strength)**
- **Hypertrophy**: increase muscle size
- **Intensity**: train the nervous system to recruit those muscles for maximal force production
- Target muscles: hips, hamstrings, calves, quads
- Lift 2–3x per week
- Avoid block periodisation (pure strength → pure jumping with nothing in between); use variety instead

**Phase 2 — Build the Springs (Tendons & Nervous System)**
- Train the **stretch-shortening cycle**: fast eccentric → fast concentric action under max intent
- Tendons store and release elastic energy; forces during jumping can exceed 10× body weight
- Build jump volume gradually:
  - Start at 1 session/week
  - Increase volume ~10% per week
  - Progress toward 2 sessions/week (elite athletes); 3–4 sessions/week possible for younger athletes only after months of build-up

**Phase 3 — Supercharge with the Force-Velocity Bridge**
- Problem: a heavy squat takes several seconds; ground contact during jumping is ~0.2 seconds
- Being strong but slow at generating force leaves vertical inches on the table
- Solution: **Olympic lifts, specifically power cleans**
  - Ground contact time mirrors the double-knee-bend timing
  - Motor pattern of first pull → double-knee-bend mimics the penultimate step → plant
  - Eccentric overload from catching a heavy clean drives unique adaptation
- Study by Satiar Ferogi (n > 1,000 athletes): power clean had the highest correlation with vertical jump of any weight room exercise

### The Force-Velocity Curve
- Heavier load = lower movement velocity
- Exercises can be mapped along this spectrum to bridge max strength and explosive jumping
- "Surfing the force-velocity curve" = using varied exercises across the spectrum to avoid plateaus

### Vertical Jump Deficit Training
- Three distinct athlete weaknesses:
  1. Strong but slow (velocity deficit)
  2. Fast but weak (strength deficit)
  3. Poor jump technique
- Identify your deficit first and address that specific weakness

### Long-Term Timeline (from Mike Young research)
- Elite athletes' vertical PRs occur on average **~5 years after squat PR** and **1–2 years after power clean PR**
- Tendons, nervous system, and muscles continue adapting even without visible PRs

---

## Actionable Advice

- **Prioritise compound strength work** (squats, hip-dominant lifts) 2–3x/week before adding high jump volume
- **Learn and implement power cleans** — teachable to beginners in ~20 minutes; directly bridges strength and jumping
- **Start jump/plyometric sessions at once per week**, then increase volume by ~10% weekly
- **Practise max-effort dunks or attempts** on a lowered or standard rim — the intent itself drives stretch-shortening cycle adaptation
- **Identify your deficit** (strength, velocity, or technique) and train that weakness first before adding volume elsewhere
- **Watch oldest-to-newest progressions** of elite athletes (e.g., Jordan Kilganon) to internalise that elite performers also started unable to dunk

---

## Things to Avoid

- **Block periodisation**: finishing all strength work and then switching entirely to jumping — leaves a critical performance gap
- **Skipping the force-velocity bridge**: going straight from heavy lifting to jump practice without Olympic lift or speed-strength work
- **Jumping heavily without building a strength base first**: leads to overuse injuries (e.g., knee pain from excessive jumping before adequate strength development)
- **Using taller athletes as strength benchmarks**: shorter athletes need greater relative strength to achieve comparable verticals
- **Quitting during plateaus**: multi-year plateaus are normal even at elite levels; PRs often follow immediately after

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Vertical improvement requires three distinct training elements**: maximal strength (engine), tendon/SSC development (springs), and force-velocity bridging via Olympic lifts — skipping any phase leaves significant gains unrealised
- **Power cleans are the single highest-correlated weight room exercise for vertical jump**, validated by a 1,000+ athlete study
- **Short athletes must be proportionally stronger** than taller peers to reach equivalent jump heights
- **Plateaus lasting years are normal** — elite PRs statistically follow squat and power clean PRs by years, so continued training is never wasted
- **Intent during jumping matters**: max-effort attempts — even failed ones — are what drive stretch-shortening cycle and tendon adaptation
